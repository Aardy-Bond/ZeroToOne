The Kestrel Light — Part One

The light on Kestrel Point had burned every night for sixty-one years, and for
the last nine of them Ilse Vary had been the one to burn it. She climbed the
hundred and four steps at dusk, trimmed the wick, wound the clock, and came
down again in the dark without a lamp, because she knew the stairs better than
she knew her own hands.

She could not swim. Everyone on the Point knew it and nobody remarked on it any
more, the way nobody remarked that the baker was deaf in one ear. Ilse had been
pulled out of the harbour at six years old, blue to the lips, and had not
willingly put her feet in salt water since. It was the private joke of the
village that the woman who kept nine hundred sailors off the rocks each winter
would not walk out past her own ankles.

Her husband had not shared the fear. Anselm Vary had gone out in the January
gale three winters ago, in a boat he had been told twice not to take, and the
sea had kept him. They had found the boat at Skerry Head with the oars still
shipped. They had never found Anselm. The parish had entered him in the book as
drowned, and the minister had said the words over an empty box, and Ilse had
climbed the hundred and four steps that same evening and lit the lamp as she
always did, because the ships did not care what the sea had taken from her.

The lamp-room key hung on her belt. There was one key and it was that one; the
Board of Trade had issued it in her name when she took the post, and the
locksmith at Arden had died the year after without leaving his patterns, so
there would be no other. She was aware of the weight of it against her hip all
day, the way another woman might be aware of a wedding ring.

In the room below the lamp she kept the ledger. It was a long grey book, bound
in oilcloth, and in it were written every wreck, every light-failure, and every
vessel that had passed the Point in sixty-one years, in the hands of four
keepers. Her own hand was the fourth. She wrote in it each morning before she
slept, and she was careful, because a keeper who is careless with the ledger is
a keeper who is hiding something.

On the ninth of October she wrote: *Wind SW, moderating. One collier north-bound,
riding light. Lamp burned clean. Nothing further.*

It was the last entry she would make for some time in which nothing further was
true.
