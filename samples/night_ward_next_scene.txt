INT. SHIRWAL COTTAGE HOSPITAL - OUTSIDE WARD FOUR - NIGHT

The brass key is warm because Revati has been holding it for an hour without using it.

The padlock is stiff. It takes both hands and a sound she is glad nobody is awake to hear. The hasp swings loose. Eleven months of held air comes out around the door frame, and it smells of wet plaster and, underneath that, of burning.

REVATI
(to herself)
Six in. Five out.

She goes in.


INT. WARD FOUR - CONTINUOUS

Her torch finds the ceiling first. Smoke has drawn a black tide-line across it, waist-high on the walls, as though the room had been filled and then drained.

Six beds. Five of them stripped to the frame, mattresses rolled and tied at the foot.

The sixth is made.

Sheet turned down. Pillow dented. A steel tray on the locker with a glass on it, and in the glass an inch of water.

NARRATOR (V.O.)
Water evaporates. She knew that the way you know a thing you have never once had to use.

She puts the back of her hand against the sheet. Then she puts her palm against it and leaves it there.


INT. SHIRWAL COTTAGE HOSPITAL - RECORDS ROOM - LATER

Revati has the August register open again, and beside it the ward stock ledger, and beside that the linen requisitions going back three years.

The linen book is the one nobody thinks about. Every ward draws sheets by the dozen and signs for them at the end of the month, and the counts are entered by the ward boy and countersigned by the matron, and the totals are carried forward to the district office in Satara, where they are entered again against the sanctioned bed strength, which for a cottage hospital of this size is forty-two.

She checks the figures for Ward Four from the previous financial year, and then the year before that, and then the months after the fire, when the ward was supposed to be empty and drawing nothing at all.

The ward has been drawing two sheets a week for eleven months. Signed by Ganpat. Countersigned by Sulochana.

She sits with that for a while. Then she works back through the requisitions to the ninth of August and checks the count for that week, and then the discharge summaries, and then the register once more, in case the register has changed since the last time she read it, which it has not.


INT. SHIRWAL COTTAGE HOSPITAL - KITCHEN PASSAGE - NIGHT

Ganpat is washing a single glass at the sink. He does it carefully, the way he does everything.

REVATI
Two sheets a week.

He does not turn around.

REVATI (CONT'D)
For a ward with nobody in it.

GANPAT
The damp gets into linen. You leave it folded eleven months, it comes out spotted, and then the district asks why you are condemning stock.

REVATI
You change the bed.

GANPAT
I air the bed.

REVATI
You change the bed, Ganpat. There was water in the glass.

Ganpat turns the tap off. In the quiet the fan is very loud.

GANPAT
You should put the key back on her chain.


INT. WARD FOUR - NIGHT

Revati goes back with the torch and this time she takes the chart from the hook at the foot of the sixth bed.

It is a standard inpatient chart, and it has been kept. Temperature, pulse, and respiration, twice daily, in a column that runs down the page and onto a second sheet clipped behind it.

She turns to the last entry.

The observations are recorded in a steady hand, and the date beside them is today's.

The name at the top of the chart has been scratched through, hard enough to go through the paper.

Underneath the scratching, where the nib has torn it open, she can read three letters of a surname.

They are hers.
