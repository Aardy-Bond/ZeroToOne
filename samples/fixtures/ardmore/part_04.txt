The woman who came for the bicycle came in the last week of October, off the
noon boat, and she went straight up the pier to the rail with a key already in
her hand, which is how Nuala knew before she had crossed the slip.

Her name was Aoife Trant and she taught at the school in Ballymacart, and in the
middle of September she had ridden down to catch the six o'clock and had missed
it by four minutes.

"By four minutes," Nuala said.

"I watched you go," said Aoife Trant. "I stood at the top there and I watched
you go and I was so angry I left the bicycle where it was and got the bus, and
then I was in Cork for six weeks with my sister, who was ill, and every week I
thought about that bicycle and every week I did nothing about it."

Nuala did not say anything about the watch, or about the four minutes, or about
her father, who had run the six o'clock by that watch for forty years and had
never once in his life waited for anybody. She thought that if Aoife Trant had
come down the pier one minute earlier in September she would have caught the
boat, and that the woman would then never have gone to Cork, and that her sister
would have been ill just the same.

Aoife Trant put her key in the padlock and turned it and lifted the bicycle away
from the rail. The drive chain had rusted into one piece. It took the two of
them twenty minutes and a tin of oil to get it turning, and then Aoife Trant
rode it up the hill in the rain, and the rail at the top of the pier was bare
for the first time since summer.
