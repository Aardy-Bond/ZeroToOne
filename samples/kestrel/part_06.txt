The Kestrel Light — Part Six

The Marisa came onto the Skerry at four in the morning on the twenty-sixth, in a
rising sea, with no light on the Point to tell her where the rocks were.

Ilse heard her strike from the cottage. It is not a sound that can be mistaken
for anything else, and it does not stop; a vessel does not hit the Skerry once,
she hits it and then goes on hitting it for as long as she holds together.

She ran. Down the cliff path, down the eleven cut steps, out onto the shingle,
and the boat that should have been there was not there, because Bengt Rask had
taken it out at midnight and had not said where.

There were lamps out on the water where the Marisa was breaking up. There were
men in the water between the Marisa and the shore, and the distance from the
shingle to the nearest of them was perhaps ninety yards, and the tide was
running out.

Ilse Vary went into the sea.

She went in badly, the way a person goes in who has spent forty years arranging
her life so as not to, and the cold took her breath entirely for the first ten
yards. She could not swim well. She had never swum. But she had spent nine years
watching this water and she knew, in the way she knew the hundred and four
steps, exactly where the outgoing tide ran and exactly where it did not, and she
went out along the slack water inside the bar with her chin up and her arms
going anyhow, and she reached the first man and turned him onto his back and
brought him in.

Then she went out again.

She brought in four. The fifth she could not find. Toivo Rask brought in two
more from the eastern end after the boat came back, and Bengt Rask, when the
boat came back, was not in it.

At half past five, with the sky going grey and eleven survivors laid out in the
lee of the store, the light came on.

Ilse was on the shingle and she saw the beam go out over the water and reach
Skerry Head and touch the white stone above the cut, clean, the way it had not
done since the tenth of October. She turned and looked up at the gallery, and
there was a figure on it, and it was not tall enough to be Toivo and it was
standing with its weight on one foot.

Anselm's boy had gone up the hundred and four steps in the dark, with a key he
had not been given, and had lit the lamp.
