The Kestrel Light — Part Three

To understand what happened on the Point that autumn it is necessary to
understand how the Point came to have a light at all, and that is a longer story
than most people in Arden could tell you.

The first light on Kestrel Point was not a lighthouse but a brazier, set on the
headland in 1798 by the parish at the insistence of a wool merchant named Dunmar
who had lost two cargoes on the Skerry in a single season. It was a coal fire in
an iron basket and it was tended by whichever cottager could be paid to sit with
it, and it went out more nights than it burned. The parish records for those
years are largely a record of arguments about who owed what for the coal.

In 1834 the Board of Trade surveyed the coast between Arden and the northern
harbours and recommended a tower. The recommendation was not acted upon for
another twenty-eight years, in part because the landowner at that time, a Colonel
Hasketh, held that a light would encourage shipping to pass closer to his
foreshore and would therefore increase the frequency of wrecks upon it, an
argument which the Board found difficult to answer because in a narrow sense it
was correct. Hasketh died in 1859. The tower was begun in 1861 and lit in 1863.

The tower is ninety-two feet from the base to the gallery, built of granite
brought by sea from the northern quarries, and its walls at the foot are nine
feet thick. There have been four keepers. The first was a man named Corrance who
kept it for thirty-one years and wrote almost nothing in the ledger beyond the
weather. The second was his son, who kept it for four years and left after the
winter of 1898. The third was Piotr Karel, who came from the northern harbours
and who kept it for seventeen years and who, in that time, wrote the fullest
entries the ledger contains, including a long account of the Marisa wreck which
is still the standard account. The fourth is Ilse Vary.

The lamp itself was converted from oil to a pressurised burner in 1902, then
converted back in 1907 after the pressurised system proved unreliable in cold
weather, and the fittings from that period are still in the store below the
light-room, along with the older brass, the spare glass, and the second key.

The arrangement of the buildings is simple. The tower stands at the seaward
point. Attached to its landward side is the keeper's cottage, two rooms and a
scullery. Behind that, at a distance of perhaps forty yards, is the store. A
path runs from the store down to the shingle by way of eleven cut steps.

ON THE HARBOUR WALL
The parish maintains a bell, rung in fog, which has hung there since 1871.

It is worth saying plainly that in the sixty-one years the tower had been lit,
the light had failed on four occasions, and on every one of those occasions the
cause had been established within a day: twice a fouled wick, once a cracked
reservoir, once a gale that broke the gallery glass. The failure that began on
the eleventh of October was the fifth, and it was the first that nobody could
account for.
