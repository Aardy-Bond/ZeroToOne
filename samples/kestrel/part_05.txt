The Kestrel Light — Part Five

The coat came ashore on the twenty-second, at the same place as the tin case,
which was not a coincidence and which Ilse understood was not a coincidence the
moment she saw where it lay.

It was a heavy pilot coat, wool, sea-ruined, and it had been in the water a long
time — long enough that the wool had gone the colour of wet ash and the horn
buttons had clouded. There was no body in it and no body near it. Toivo Rask
found it and would not touch it, and came up the cliff path to fetch her instead.

She went through the pockets on the shingle with the tide coming in.

In the left pocket there was nothing. In the right there was a fold of oiled
paper containing four matches, useless. Sewn into the lining, under the arm,
where a man puts a thing he intends to keep, there was a key.

It was brass, worn smooth, and it was cut to the lamp-room lock.

She sat back on her heels in the shingle and held it in her palm and understood
several things at once, in the wrong order, the way one does.

There was a second key. There had been a second key the whole time. The
locksmith at Arden had died without leaving his patterns, and the Board had
issued one key in her name, and both of those things were true and neither of
them meant what she had taken them to mean, because the key in her hand had not
been cut after the Board issued hers. It had been cut before. It was older than
her appointment. It had the square shoulder that the Arden locksmith had put on
everything he made until he changed his pattern in 1901.

Someone had been able to enter the lamp-room since before she had ever climbed
the hundred and four steps.

She thought about the beam falling short of Skerry Head by four hundred yards,
and then by six hundred, and then not reaching at all. Nothing wrong with the
burner. Nothing wrong with the wick. Nothing wrong with the oil in the pail she
had held a lantern over.

Nothing wrong with the oil in the pail.

The bell on the harbour wall had rung for eleven minutes on the night Anselm went
out in the January gale, and the parish had always said it was rung for the
weather. It was not rung for the weather. It was rung by someone who wanted a
boat to come in to a particular part of the harbour at a particular hour, and it
had worked, and the boat had come in, and the boat had gone out again with one
more man aboard than it had carried in.

She had not asked why the bell rang. In three years it had not once occurred to
her to ask.

That, she thought, is how it was done. Not the light. The light is the second
thing. The light is what you break when someone has begun to look at the first
thing.
