The Kestrel Light — Part Four

On the nineteenth the light went out altogether.

Ilse was on the gallery when it happened and she felt it before she saw it — the
particular quality of dark that comes when a thing you have not been looking at
stops being there. The flame did not go down. It went from burning to not
burning between one breath and the next, and there was no smoke.

She relit it four times that night. It burned for nine minutes, then six, then
four, then not at all.

At first light she went down to the store for the older brass, and the ledger was
not in the light-room where she had left it.

She searched for two hours with a thoroughness that frightened her. It was not
in the light-room, not in the store, not in the cottage, not under the bed nor
behind the press nor in the coal. The ledger was missing, and a keeper who loses
the ledger loses the post, and she sat down on the eleven cut steps above the
shingle and did not go up again until the tide had turned.

By noon the harbour master had come out from Arden with three men, and by two
o'clock they were standing in the yard behind the cottage taking the roll of
everyone who had been on the Point in the last month, because a light that will
not burn and a book that has walked off are, taken together, a matter for the
Board.

The harbour master read the names off his list in the flat voice of a man who
has done this before.

"Karel, Piotr. Deceased. Vary, Ilse. Present." He went on down the sheet.
"Toivo Rask, present. Bengt Rask, present. Anselm Vary."

"Here," said a young man at the back of the yard.

Ilse turned around. He was nineteen or twenty, dark, standing with his weight on
one foot in a way she had not seen in three years and had stopped expecting to
see again. Anselm's boy. Her husband's son by his first wife, who had been
raised in the northern harbours by an aunt and who had last stood on the Point
as a child of nine.

"You have his name," she said.

"I have had it the whole time," said the young man.

They found the ledger that evening, in the store, wrapped in oilcloth inside a
crate of spare glass that had not been opened since 1907. Nothing had been torn
out. Nothing had been added. It had simply been put where nobody would look, by
someone who had known where nobody would look.

It had been missing for eleven hours and the harbour master wrote that down, and
Ilse wrote it in the ledger itself once she had it back, which struck her as
absurd even as she did it: a book recording its own absence.
