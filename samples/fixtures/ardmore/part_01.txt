Nuala Deane had run the ferry at Ardmore for eleven years, since the winter her
father stopped being able to hold the wheel. Brendan Deane had died two years
ago this February, in the upstairs room of the house on Quay Street, and Nuala
had buried him in the churchyard above the harbour where he could be rained on
by the same weather that had made him.

She kept his watch. It was a steel Smiths with a scratched crystal and it ran
four minutes fast, and she had never had it seen to, because four minutes fast
was how her father had kept it and how he had run the crossing his whole life.
She wore it on the inside of her wrist, the way he had.

The ferry went across at eight, at noon, and at six, and back again on the half
hour. In eleven years Nuala had cancelled the evening crossing once, in the
storm of the January before last, when the swell at the harbour mouth stood up
green and the harbour master had come down to the slip himself to tell her not
to be a fool about it.

There was a bicycle chained to the rail at the top of the pier. It was a woman's
bicycle, black, with a basket on the front and a good deal of rust in the chain,
and it had been there since the middle of September. Nobody had claimed it.
Nuala had asked at the shop and at the post office and nobody knew whose it was.
She had begun, without quite deciding to, to look for its owner among the faces
that came off the boat.
