The Kestrel Light — Part Two

The lamp began to fail on the eleventh.

It did not fail the way she had been trained to expect. There was no guttering,
no smell of a fouled wick, no slow browning of the flame that meant water in the
oil. It simply gave less light. She stood on the gallery in the cold with her
back to the glass and watched the beam reach out towards Skerry Head and stop
short of it, the way a voice stops short when the speaker has lost their nerve.

She measured it against the marks on the headland. On a clean night the beam
touched the white stone above the Skerry cut. That night it fell four hundred
yards inside it, and the next night a little further in again.

She stripped the burner and found nothing wrong with the burner. She replaced
the wick and the new wick burned exactly as badly as the old one. She drained
the reservoir into a pail and held a lantern over it and the oil was clear and
smelled as it should. She wrote all of this in the ledger in her careful fourth
hand, and underneath it she wrote a sentence that was not a report of anything:
*I do not know why.*

On the thirteenth the sea gave up a manifest.

It came ashore in the shingle below the cliff path, folded twice inside a tin
tobacco case, and the case had been sealed with candle-wax and had kept the
paper almost dry. It was a cargo manifest for a vessel that was not named on it,
listing forty tons of pig iron, eleven casks of oil, and a consignment of
window-glass for a buyer in Arden. It was dated the second of October, nine days
before her lamp began to dim.

At the foot of it there was a signature.

Ilse took it up to the light-room and laid it flat under the good lamp and
turned it every way she could, and she could not read it. It was not a name that
had been smudged by water; the paper around it was dry and unmarked. It was a
name that had been written by someone who did not want it read — a single
continuous mark, looping back over itself twice, ending in a downstroke that
tore the paper.

She had seen every manifest that had come ashore on this coast in nine years.
She had never seen a master decline to sign his own name.

Who signed it? She wrote the question in the ledger, which was not what the
ledger was for, and she underlined it, which was worse.

Then she went down the hundred and four steps in the dark, and for the first
time in nine years she counted them, because she was no longer certain the
number would be the same at the bottom.
