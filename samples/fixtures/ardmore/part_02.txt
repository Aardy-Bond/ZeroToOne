The noon crossing on the Tuesday carried four passengers and a crate of day-old
chicks for the farm at Corrin. One of the four was a man Nuala had not seen
before, forty or so, with a canvas bag and hands that had done something with
them. He gave his name as Cathal Rynne when she asked it for the book, and he
stood at the rail the whole way over instead of sitting in the cabin, which she
liked him for.

Halfway across, where the channel narrows and the water goes from grey to a
deeper grey, Nuala put the wheel over two spokes without thinking about it. She
could hear her father as clearly as if he had been standing at her shoulder.

"Not there," Brendan said. "You'll feel it want to go and you'll let it. There's
a shoulder of rock under that and it has taken better than you."

"I know where the rock is," she had told him, sixteen years old and furious.

"You know where it is on the chart," he had said. "That's a different thing to
knowing where it is."

She had been thirty-one before she understood what he meant, and by then he was
past being told so. She held the wheel over the two spokes and the ferry came
through the narrows the way it always did, and Cathal Rynne at the rail never
knew there had been anything under him at all.

The engine coughed twice on the run in to the far slip. It had been doing that
since August. Nuala had a man in Youghal who could look at it and had not yet
found a week when she could spare the boat.
